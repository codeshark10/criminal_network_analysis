# DETAILED CASE FILE
## Punjab National Bank Letter-of-Undertaking (LoU) Fraud — "The Nirav Modi–Mehul Choksi Case"

**Case also known as:** PNB Scam, PNB Brady House Fraud, PNB LoU Scam, ₹13,000–14,000 crore PNB fraud
**Complainant Institution:** Punjab National Bank, Brady House Branch, Fort, Mumbai (`O009`)
**Investigating Agencies:** Central Bureau of Investigation (CBI) — Anti-Corruption Branch, Mumbai; Enforcement Directorate (ED) — Mumbai Zone (Prevention of Money Laundering Act, 2002)
**Reported Quantum:** approximately ₹13,000–14,000 crore (~US$2 billion); the two original FIRs together total ₹11,384.92 crore (₹6,498.20 cr + ₹4,886.72 cr), with the higher headline figure reflecting the full scale established as investigation progressed

---

## 1. Executive Summary

Between 2011 and January 2018, officials at Punjab National Bank's Brady House branch in Mumbai fraudulently issued Letters of Undertaking (LoUs) — bank guarantees used to raise short-term "buyers' credit" financing for imports — on behalf of firms controlled by diamond jeweller Nirav Modi (`P001`) and his uncle Mehul Choksi (`P004`), without sanctioned credit limits, collateral margin, or entries in the bank's core banking system. The guarantees were transmitted over the SWIFT interbank messaging network to overseas branches of other Indian banks, which lent against them in good faith. Because the LoUs never appeared in PNB's internal ledgers, new guarantees were routinely raised to repay the buyers'-credit obligations created by earlier ones — an "evergreening" pattern that let the exposure balloon for years without detection. The fraud surfaced in January 2018 when the applicant firms sought a fresh LoU without the usual cash margin from bank staff unfamiliar with the informal arrangement, and were refused. Both principal accused had already left India days earlier. What followed became India's largest bank fraud case: two CBI FIRs, parallel ED money-laundering cases, Interpol Red Corner Notices, the country's first use of the Fugitive Economic Offenders Act, the collapse and liquidation of two major listed companies, and extradition litigation in the UK and Belgium that is still working its way to a physical handover as of this writing.

---

## 2. Background — How a Letter of Undertaking Fraud Works

A **Letter of Undertaking (LoU)** is a bank guarantee that lets an Indian importer obtain short-term foreign-currency financing — "buyers' credit" — from the overseas branch of another bank, with the issuing bank guaranteeing repayment. Ordinarily, an LoU requires: (a) a sanctioned credit limit for the applicant, (b) cash margin or approved collateral, and (c) an entry in the issuing bank's Core Banking System (CBS — PNB used Finacle) so the exposure is tracked and reconciled.

LoUs are transmitted to the correspondent bank's overseas branch via **SWIFT**, the global interbank messaging network. Critically, at the time, PNB's Brady House branch had **no integration between its SWIFT terminal and its Core Banking System** — a SWIFT message could be sent without any corresponding CBS entry, and without the multi-level authorisation a normal loan would require. This gap is what the fraud exploited.

## 3. Modus Operandi — Step by Step

1. **Password-level access.** Gokulnath Shetty (`P005`), then Deputy Manager at the Brady House branch, is alleged to have obtained an unauthorised SWIFT password with Level-5 (authorising) rights — a level of access well beyond his sanctioned role — reportedly by exploiting weak internal password-rotation controls after a colleague's transfer.
2. **Off-books LoU issuance.** Working with Single Window Operator Manoj Kharat (`P006`), Shetty issued LoUs on SWIFT directly to the overseas branches of client banks (mainly other Indian banks' Hong Kong, Dubai, Antwerp and Frankfurt branches), naming firms controlled by Modi and Choksi as beneficiaries, **without** creating a matching Finacle/CBS entry, so no internal red flag was ever raised.
3. **No collateral, no sanction.** Because the transactions never touched the CBS, the mandatory 100% cash margin and credit-committee sanction that would normally accompany an LoU were never obtained.
4. **Drawdown.** The receiving overseas bank branch, trusting the SWIFT-authenticated guarantee, extended buyers'-credit loans to the applicant firms — Diamond R US (`O001`), Solar Exports (`O002`), Stellar Diamonds (`O003`) for the Modi group, and Gitanjali Gems (`O005`) and related entities for the Choksi group.
5. **Evergreening.** As each LoU's buyers'-credit obligation neared maturity, a **new** LoU was issued to raise fresh financing, part of which was used to settle the prior obligation. This rollover pattern let liabilities compound for roughly seven years without ever surfacing on PNB's books. (This is the pattern encoded in the `prior_lou_reference` field of `edges_financial.csv`.)
6. **Alleged bribery conduit.** A broker, Debajyoti Dutta (`P012`) of Rishika Financials (`O008`), is alleged to have shopped competitive LoU-funding quotes to overseas bank branches on behalf of the Gitanjali group and taken a brokerage of roughly 0.05% of the LoU value, a portion of which — reported at approximately ₹1.08 crore between 2014 and 2017 — was allegedly passed on to Shetty.
7. **Detection.** Shetty retired in May 2017. In January 2018, when the applicant firms sought a further LoU without margin money, the new officials at the branch — unaware of the prior arrangement — declined, and the fraud came to light. By then, roughly 150 fraudulent LoUs are reported to have been issued since 2011.
8. **Flight.** Nirav Modi and his family reportedly left India around January 1, 2018, and Mehul Choksi around January 4, 2018 — in both cases before the fraud became public knowledge.

## 6. Investigating Agencies, Charges and Court Proceedings

- **CBI** registered the two FIRs described above under Sections 120-B (criminal conspiracy), 420 (cheating), 409 (criminal breach of trust by a public servant/banker), 467, 468, 471 (forgery-related offences) of the Indian Penal Code, 1860, read with Section 13(2) r/w 13(1)(d) of the Prevention of Corruption Act, 1988 — reflecting both the fraud itself and the alleged corrupt conduct of bank officials. A first chargesheet was filed in May 2018, with supplementary chargesheets filed in the following years as the investigation identified further transactions and additional accused.
- **ED** opened parallel Enforcement Case Information Reports (ECIRs) under the Prevention of Money Laundering Act, 2002, treating the fraud proceeds as the predicate offence for a money-laundering investigation, and filed its own chargesheets (Prosecution Complaints) in May 2018.
- The matter is tried before a **Special PMLA/CBI Court in Mumbai**, which has also handled the Fugitive Economic Offender proceedings against both principal accused.

## 8. Fugitive Economic Offenders Act (FEOA), 2018

The FEOA was enacted in 2018 specifically to allow confiscation of a fugitive's assets when they flee India to avoid facing economic-offence charges and refuse to return despite summons. Nirav Modi became the **first person declared a Fugitive Economic Offender** under this Act, by a special PMLA court in Mumbai in January 2019, triggering confiscation proceedings against his assets. Mehul Choksi faced parallel FEO proceedings; on November 29, 2025, a Mumbai special court rejected his plea to have those proceedings dropped, keeping the domestic confiscation track active alongside the Belgian extradition case.

## 9. International Legal Proceedings — Nirav Modi (United Kingdom)

- **Mar 2019:** Arrested in London on an Indian extradition warrant; held at HMP Wandsworth, a category-B men's prison.
- **Feb 25, 2021:** Westminster Magistrates' Court rules in favour of extradition on all grounds — fraud, money laundering, and no human-rights bar to a fair trial in India.
- **2021–2022:** Modi appeals on mental-health grounds, arguing a high risk of suicide if extradited.
- **Nov 9, 2022:** UK High Court dismisses the appeal, finding that safeguards at Arthur Road Jail, Mumbai would adequately manage his condition.
- **Dec 15, 2022:** High Court refuses permission to appeal further to the UK Supreme Court, effectively exhausting his ordinary domestic remedies; he is left with the option of an application to the European Court of Human Rights.
- **Since then:** Modi has remained in custody in the UK; a UK court has more recently denied him bail while the final steps toward physical extradition are pending, and no confirmed transfer to India has been reported as of this writing.

## 10. International Legal Proceedings — Mehul Choksi (Antigua & Barbuda, Dominica, Belgium)

- **Jan 2018:** Flees India for Antigua, having obtained citizenship there in late 2017 under its Citizenship-by-Investment programme; takes the citizenship oath Jan 15, 2018.

- **Nov 29, 2024:** Antwerp district court's pre-trial chamber rules the Indian arrest warrants enforceable.
- **Apr 3, 2025:** Antwerp Court of Appeal's chamber of accusation gives a positive advisory opinion on extradition, on six of seven counts, finding the alleged offences satisfy Belgium's dual-criminality requirement.
- **Apr 11–12, 2025:** Choksi is formally arrested by Antwerp police.
- **Sep 2025:** Extradition hearings proceed; India formally assures Belgium of humane custody conditions at Arthur Road Jail.

## 11. Corporate Insolvency Proceedings

## 12. Regulatory and Systemic Reforms Following the Case

- **March 13, 2018:** The Reserve Bank of India barred Indian banks from issuing Letters of Undertaking and Letters of Comfort for trade-credit financing of imports into India, closing off the specific instrument the fraud relied on (standby letters of credit remain permitted in limited circumstances).
- Banks were separately pushed toward tighter integration between SWIFT messaging terminals and each branch's Core Banking System, so that any SWIFT-originated guarantee automatically creates a corresponding CBS entry and cannot bypass sanction/collateral checks — directly closing the specific control gap this fraud exploited.
- The case is widely cited as the direct trigger for the **Fugitive Economic Offenders Act, 2018**, enacted later that year.
